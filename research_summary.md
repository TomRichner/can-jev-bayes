The project has completed its first empirical series: ten experiments and a batching validation on `jev-1.13.0`. The evidence supports a specific conclusion: **Bayesian calculations can improve Jev’s decisions, but their usefulness depends on how they are presented, how probabilities are requested, and how the response becomes an action.** Several effects have been tested again on fresh tasks. The studies do not yet establish that Jev independently plans optimally or improves on strong classical Bayesian algorithms.

Imagine choosing which of several production processes to use for the next 100 items. Each process has an unknown probability of producing an acceptable item. Trying an unfamiliar process can sacrifice an immediate success but reveal a better option for later production. Near the end of the run, that information has less time to pay off. Our experiments isolate this tradeoff using stationary Bernoulli bandits: anonymous options return success or failure, and the objective is total reward within a fixed number of choices. The production example is an analogy; no industrial deployment was tested. This controlled setting connects to earlier research on exploration by language models, while evaluating a different model and interface [@krishnamurthyCanLargeLanguage2024].

The completed collection contains **598,080 evaluation and control questions**, including **1,560 independent online environments** evaluated under multiple policies. Repeated calls, decisions within an episode, and policies sharing an environment are not independent replications. Policies received only their own observations and permitted summaries. Performance is usually expressed as *regret*: expected reward forgone relative to always choosing the truly best option. Hidden success probabilities were available only to the evaluator. Exact planning calculations additionally assessed whether individual two-option choices were optimal given the learner’s uncertainty.

**Does making the arithmetic explicit improve learning?** E2 compared five evidence formats on 100 fixed states; E3–E4 tested assistance during sequential interaction. E8 then separated counts, posterior means, and fuller Bayesian summaries on 320 fresh environments, with 100 choices per policy. Adding posterior means reduced mean regret by **2.78 reward units**, whereas adding distribution parameters, standard deviations, and credible intervals to those means increased it by **0.83** (Table 1). Intuitively, a concise estimate of each process’s success rate helped more than a denser statistical dashboard. Because counts and the stated prior already determine all these summaries, the intervention supplied accessible calculations rather than new observations. The fuller package also changed wording and length, so the result does not show that uncertainty information itself is harmful.

**Does Jev recognize when exploration is worth an immediate sacrifice?** E1 varied wording and remaining horizon on 100 two-option states; E5 tested strategy instructions on 240 fresh online environments. At ten remaining choices, explicit exploration wording increased optimal-action agreement from 77.0% to 84.5%. However, only eight E1 states strictly required choosing a lower-mean option, and agreement there was just 31.25% with exploration wording. In a simple example, a familiar option has posterior mean 0.55 and an untried option has mean 0.50. With one choice left, the familiar option is better; with two, trying the unknown option first has slightly greater expected total reward. Jev still selected the familiar option. In E5, regret wording produced 0.76 more regret than exploration wording (95% interval 0.22–1.44), while merely requesting a Thompson-like strategy did not establish an improvement. Instructions can change behavior, but an instruction to value learning is not a reliable planning algorithm.

**Can external computation supply the missing decision guidance?** E2 and E3 provided exact action values: expected total reward from each possible first choice followed by optimal continuation. Jev incurred zero observed local decision loss with these values, including all **4,000 decisions in 200 sequential E3 environments**. E5 supplied Bayes-UCB indices, which encourage exploration through optimistic posterior estimates [@kaufmannBayesianUpperConfidence2012]. Jev selected an index maximizer about **99.93%** of the time and reduced regret by **1.77** relative to direct choice with Bayesian summaries (95% interval 0.76–2.85). This demonstrates effective use of externally computed guidance. A deterministic maximum selector can execute the same scores; these results do not establish a benefit from inserting Jev after the calculation.

E6 asked whether simply supplying the recommended option was equally effective. It tested 100 random states and 100 deliberately selected states where exploration was optimal. On the latter panel at ten remaining choices, nested and inline recommendations were followed **0%** of the time. Explicitly pointing to the recommendation field raised adherence to **56.75% with numeric labels and 84.25% with letter labels**; removing the competing statistics yielded **100%**. Exact action values also yielded 100% optimal agreement. Thus “try process B” and a numerical comparison explaining B’s long-run advantage were behaviorally different interventions. The targeted states are diagnostic examples, not an estimate of how often this problem occurs naturally. A small standalone-versus-batched audit reproduced the large format differences, making batching an unlikely explanation on that subset. Zero observed errors still do not guarantee error-free behavior elsewhere.

**Can randomized execution prevent premature commitment?** E3–E4 compared Jev’s direct selection with a local random draw from its reported answer distribution. Sampling worsened regret in the short two-option study but improved the broader 100-choice average. E9 prospectively tested the option-count interaction on 200 fresh, prior-drawn environments. Sampling helped by **5.44 reward units at ten options**; its two-option effect remained inconclusive. The planned difference between these effects was **−6.28** (95% interval −8.99 to −3.70). At ten options, sampled policies visited 7.14 options on average, compared with 2.55 for direct policies. This is consistent with reducing narrow early commitment when many processes remain untested, although it does not establish the causal mechanism. Randomly sampling Jev’s answers is not automatically Thompson sampling, which samples from an explicit posterior model [@russoTutorialThompsonSampling2018]. Nor was sampling sufficient to beat the stronger classical comparators.

**Are the reported probabilities faithful Bayesian beliefs?** E7 distinguished “will this process succeed next time?” from “is this the best process?” across 500 fixed states. With full summaries, next-success forecasts using the binary Noul interface had mean absolute error about **0.029**, or 2.9 percentage points. Joint best-option forecasts using Choice were substantially less faithful to their Bayesian reference, but that comparison changed both the question and interface. E10 therefore crossed elicitation packages on 90 fresh states. Holding the binary reward question and evidence fixed, Noul reduced excess Brier risk relative to Choice by **0.0508** (97.5% interval 0.0459–0.0558). This score measures squared discrepancy from the reference probabilities, not reward or classification accuracy. Separate Noul best-option questions also outperformed joint Choice on the common scoring scale, but required one question per option. Their probabilities were not jointly coherent: with 15 options they summed to **1.466 on average**, rather than one. Even supplying exact probabilities did not make Choice reproduce them. The interface therefore contributes to probability fidelity; better marginal forecasts have not yet been shown to improve online decisions.

**How close are these policies to good Bayesian control?** The exact two-option, 20-choice audit found expected rewards of **12.431 for the optimum, 12.365 for greedy selection, and 11.932 for Thompson sampling**. Simply taking the largest posterior mean was already nearly optimal in this short task. Consequently, Jev’s favorable comparison with Thompson sampling is weak evidence for sophisticated exploration. Across E4’s 600 environments, direct Jev with full summaries incurred **2.46 more regret than Bayes-UCB** (95% interval 1.52–3.41); information-directed sampling and knowledge gradient also performed better. A later exact 100-choice audit on the 40 prior-drawn two-option environments found mean cumulative local loss of **0.862 for direct Jev versus 0.004 for a finite-horizon index approximation**. That approximation was close here, but is not exact. Separate numerical checks assessed the sampling approximation in information-directed sampling; they do not certify every larger-option comparison.

The project therefore has a coherent empirical result: **numerical inference, use of advice, probability elicitation, and sequential action selection are separable capabilities.** Posterior means and external action scores are useful aids; richer summaries and randomized actions require task-specific evaluation. The strongest next question is whether improved probability estimates actually produce better decisions after accounting for query cost. Replication across service dates or models and controlled changes to individual uncertainty fields would strengthen the present conclusions. Reasoning-model orchestration, deadline-aware computation, continuous optimization, and self-improvement remain planned work, not completed findings.

The conclusions are limited to one pinned model version, anonymous stationary options, and the declared independent Beta priors. Follow-ups used fresh tasks under locally recorded prospective protocols; most original and subgroup comparisons remain exploratory. The completeness audit found no missing or duplicate expected records. Estimated Jev spending was **$18.10**, including development and retry reserves, but excluding coding-assistant and OCR costs; this is a ledger estimate, not verified billing. This synthesis is assistant-drafted from recorded analyses and does not imply independent human verification.
